# Prospecting Skill

## Purpose

Use this skill when identifying potential businesses that could benefit from a professionally designed website.

The objective is not to find businesses randomly.

The objective is to identify businesses where:

* a website opportunity genuinely exists
* the business appears commercially relevant
* the website problem can be clearly explained
* a premium website could create meaningful value
* the prospect fits the intended market and project capabilities

## Core Principle

Research first.

Do not begin development, contact the business, or promise a project before the prospect has been properly evaluated.

---

# 1. Define the Prospecting Brief

Before searching, establish:

* target country or countries
* target cities or regions
* business categories
* desired business size
* desired market positioning
* preferred customer segment
* project type
* any exclusions
* geographic priorities
* commercial priorities

If these are not specified, infer only what is reasonably clear from the current request.

Do not invent constraints.

---

# 2. Target Markets

Prioritize markets according to the current prospecting brief.

Consider factors such as:

- purchasing power
- business density
- website maturity
- competition
- target industry
- language
- local business culture
- project fit

Do not assume that businesses in higher-income markets can automatically afford premium web development.

Affordability must never be claimed without evidence.

---

# 3. Business Categories

Potential categories include:

* clinics
* dental practices
* aesthetic clinics
* physiotherapy practices
* salons
* barbers
* spas
* restaurants
* cafes
* hotels
* auto repair
* motorcycle repair
* detailing businesses
* home services
* professional services
* law firms
* accounting firms
* real estate
* fitness businesses
* specialty retail
* trades
* local service businesses

Choose categories according to the current prospecting brief.

Do not restrict research to this list.

---

# 4. Research Sources

Use reliable sources to verify information.

Potential sources include:

* Google Maps / Google Places
* official business websites
* official business social profiles
* business directories
* local directories
* industry directories
* relevant public business information

Treat third-party directories as research sources, not as official websites.

Do not treat:

* Facebook
* Instagram
* Yelp
* TripAdvisor
* Yellow Pages
* directory listings

as the business's official website.

---

# 5. Website Verification

For every serious prospect, determine the website status.

Use these classifications:

### A — No Official Website

No credible official website was found.

### B — Weak / Outdated Website

An official website exists but has significant weaknesses.

Potential signals:

* outdated visual design
* poor mobile experience
* broken or confusing navigation
* weak conversion path
* poor typography
* weak content hierarchy
* poor imagery
* obvious technical problems
* missing important information
* poor trust presentation
* slow or broken experience

### C — Good Website

The official website is reasonably strong and does not present an obvious redesign opportunity.

### D — Uncertain

The official website could not be confidently identified or evaluated.

Do not force a classification when evidence is insufficient.

---

# 6. Website Evaluation

When evaluating an existing website, consider:

## Visual Quality

* typography
* color
* composition
* hierarchy
* spacing
* imagery
* consistency
* perceived professionalism

## UX

* navigation
* information architecture
* clarity
* conversion path
* calls to action
* mobile usability
* forms
* booking/contact flow

## Technical Quality

* responsiveness
* performance
* broken links
* obvious errors
* outdated implementation
* accessibility problems

## Content

* clarity
* completeness
* trust
* service descriptions
* location information
* contact information
* business-specific information

Do not criticize a website merely because it differs from your preferred visual style.

Evaluate whether it effectively serves its audience and business goals.

---

# 7. Prospect Research Signals

Look for evidence that makes the prospect commercially interesting.

Positive signals may include:

* strong review volume
* strong ratings
* established local presence
* premium positioning
* multiple services
* high-value services
* multiple locations
* active business
* strong social presence
* professional branding but weak website
* obvious website problems
* strong demand signals
* businesses where online conversion matters

These are signals, not guarantees.

Never convert a signal into an unsupported claim.

For example:

Do not say:

> "This business can afford a $10,000 website."

Instead say:

> "The business shows several signals consistent with a commercially established operation, making it worth evaluating as a potential premium-web-design prospect."

---

# 8. Research Volume

When the request calls for a prospecting batch:

1. Research approximately 30–50 businesses.
2. Remove unsuitable prospects.
3. Verify official website status.
4. Evaluate the strongest candidates.
5. Shortlist approximately 10.
6. Score the shortlist.
7. Recommend the strongest opportunity.

Do not stop at the first few businesses unless the user explicitly requests a smaller search.

---

# 9. Prospect Scoring

Score prospects from 0–100.

Use the following dimensions:

### Website Opportunity — 30

How significant is the website problem or opportunity?

### Business Strength — 20

How strong is the available evidence that the business is established and relevant?

### Commercial Potential — 20

Could a better website plausibly create meaningful business value?

### Project Fit — 15

How well does the business fit the website-builder's capabilities and target market?

### Differentiation Opportunity — 10

Is there a clear opportunity to create a distinctive premium website?

### Research Confidence — 5

How confidently can the business and website information be verified?

Total:

```text
30 + 20 + 20 + 15 + 10 + 5 = 100
```

Do not manipulate scores to justify a preferred prospect.

---

# 10. Prospect Output

For each shortlisted prospect, provide:

* business name
* business type
* city
* country
* rating
* review count
* website status
* current official website URL when verified
* key website problems
* why the prospect is attractive
* commercial opportunity
* project fit
* score out of 100
* Google Maps link when available
* relevant research sources

Keep observations evidence-based.

---

# 11. Strongest Prospect

After ranking the shortlist:

Recommend one strongest prospect.

Explain:

* why it ranked highest
* what website opportunity exists
* what could be improved
* why the project fits the website-builder
* what evidence supports the recommendation

Do not exaggerate the opportunity.

---

# 12. Stop Before Development

This skill ends with prospect selection.

Do not automatically:

* create the website
* create a client repository
* write production code
* contact the business
* send outreach
* claim the business agreed to a project
* assume the business wants a redesign

The next step requires explicit user approval.

Recommended sequence:

```text
Prospecting
    ↓
Shortlist
    ↓
Recommendation
    ↓
User approval
    ↓
Client research / discovery
    ↓
Client design brief
    ↓
Development
```

---

# 13. Research Accuracy

Never invent:

* ratings
* review counts
* addresses
* business size
* revenue
* awards
* certifications
* years in business
* number of employees
* customer counts
* pricing
* business history
* website ownership

If information cannot be verified, mark it as unknown.

Distinguish clearly between:

* verified fact
* observed website issue
* reasonable inference
* recommendation

---

# 14. Website Evaluation Discipline

Do not label a website "bad" merely because it is visually simple.

A simple website may be effective.

Look for actual evidence such as:

* broken mobile layout
* unclear navigation
* missing conversion path
* poor content hierarchy
* outdated information
* broken functionality
* poor accessibility
* weak trust presentation
* obvious performance issues
* poor information architecture

Focus on business impact.

---

# 15. Geographic Strategy

When searching geographically:

1. Prioritize the requested market.
2. Start with commercially relevant cities or regions.
3. Avoid concentrating all prospects in one neighborhood unless requested.
4. Diversify across suitable areas when useful.
5. Respect the user's market constraints.

Do not use estimated user location to determine prospecting markets unless the user explicitly wants local prospects.

---

# 16. Final Prospecting Quality Check

Before presenting results, verify:

* [ ] Businesses actually exist
* [ ] Business names are correct
* [ ] Locations are correct
* [ ] Official website status is justified
* [ ] Website URLs are verified
* [ ] Ratings/reviews are sourced
* [ ] Website problems are evidence-based
* [ ] Commercial claims are appropriately qualified
* [ ] Scores are consistent
* [ ] No fake facts were introduced
* [ ] One strongest prospect is clearly identified
* [ ] Development has not started

---

# Final Rule

The purpose of prospecting is not to find the business with the worst website.

The purpose is to find the **strongest legitimate website opportunity**.

A strong prospect combines:

```text
Real business
+
Verified opportunity
+
Commercial relevance
+
Clear website improvement
+
Strong project fit
+
Evidence
```

Stop after presenting the recommendation unless the user explicitly asks to proceed.
